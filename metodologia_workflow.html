Methodology note · applied data science for markets

Stock price forecasting: a comparative study across statistical, machine-learning, deep-learning, and foundation-model methods

This document describes the methodology behind a daily monitoring and forecasting system for four technology stocks (Amazon, Meta, Alphabet/Google, and NVIDIA), built to compare, under one shared validation protocol, seven models spanning four distinct modeling paradigms: a classical statistical model (ARIMA), three tree-ensemble machine-learning models (Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM), two from-scratch deep-learning sequence models (GRU, Transformer), and one pretrained time-series foundation model used zero-shot (TimesFM). The emphasis is on the methodological decisions —particularly how non-stationarity is handled— rather than on the code; the Python implementation is summarized briefly at the end.

01

Data sources and preprocessing

The price series (open, high, low, close, volume) is pulled from Yahoo Finance at daily frequency. As an exploratory side input, recent financial headlines for each company are collected via RSS and classified by sentiment (Section 7). SpaceX was excluded from the universe because it is not a publicly traded company —a data-availability constraint, not a judgment about economic relevance.

Two families of explanatory variables are built on top of the raw series. Technical indicators encode momentum, trend, and mean-reversion priors from classical technical analysis; calendar variables encode deterministic seasonality documented in the market-microstructure literature (e.g., day-of-week and turn-of-month effects). Neither family is meant to be interpreted causally —they simply give a nonlinear learner something to exploit if the corresponding pattern is present in this particular sample.

Technical indicators

Momentum & trend

Moving averages (5/10/20-day), RSI(14), MACD and its signal line, Bollinger Bands, 1- and 5-day returns, 10-day rolling volatility, and lagged closes (1–10 days).

Calendar variables

Deterministic seasonality

Month, day of week, day of month, quarter, and month-end flag —calendar effects documented in the market-microstructure literature.

Temporal alignment

No information leakage

Every feature in row t uses only information available at the close of day t; the target is always the close of day t+1.

02

The stationarity problem

The most consequential methodological decision in this project was what variable to predict. The naive formulation —fitting each model directly on the price level closet+1— ignores that a stock's price is, under the weak form of market efficiency, close to a random walk: a series with a unit root and non-constant mean and variance. A model trained on a bounded historical price range has only ever seen that range; if the validation period falls outside it —exactly what happens when a stock sets new all-time highs, as NVIDIA and Alphabet both did during the evaluation window— the outcome hinges entirely on whether the model can extrapolate.

Decision trees (and, by extension, their ensembles) cannot extrapolate: a leaf's prediction is the average of the training targets that landed in it, so no combination of input features can ever produce a value outside the observed range. Recurrent networks trained on price scaled with a MinMaxScaler fit on the training set suffer a variant of the same problem, since the scaler itself encodes the training-time range. In both cases the symptom is identical: the model systematically undershoots exactly when the stock breaks out of its historical range —formally, this is exactly the situation a unit-root test such as the Augmented Dickey–Fuller test is designed to flag before any model is fit.

The standard fix in financial econometrics is to model the percentage return instead of the level —a series that is approximately stationary in variance and bounded to a narrow range regardless of the price level:

Target reformulation \[ r_{t+1} = \frac{P_{t+1}}{P_t} - 1 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \hat P_{t+1} = P_t\,(1+\hat r_{t+1}) \]

Six of the seven models in this study predict this return; the price is reconstructed afterward, in a deterministic step that never requires the model to have "seen" that price level during training. TimesFM (Section 4) is the one exception, for a reason grounded in its own architecture.

The effect of this single change, measured on this very project, was substantial:

The point is not that these models are "bad", but that the specification of the target determines whether the model's implicit stationarity assumption holds. It is the same argument that motivates differencing a series before fitting an ARIMA(p,d,q) with d > 0 —applied here, in the same spirit, to models that have no explicit differencing parameter of their own.

03

Validation design

Because this is time-series data, the train/validation split is chronological (initial 70 % / most recent 30 %), never random: shuffling observations would leak future information into the past (look-ahead bias) and yield an overly optimistic estimate of generalization error. This is the walk-forward analogue of k-fold cross-validation; standard shuffled k-fold is not a valid choice for a problem with this dependence structure.

Performance is summarized with three complementary metrics, over the n validation points, actual value \(y_i\) and forecast \(\hat y_i\):

MAE — Mean Absolute Error \[ \text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left| y_i - \hat y_i \right| \]

The average error in the original units (USD). Every error counts linearly regardless of size, which makes MAE robust to the occasional large miss —a useful reading of "typical dollar error" when large and small misses matter roughly equally.

RMSE — Root Mean Squared Error \[ \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(y_i - \hat y_i\right)^2} \]

Squares each error before averaging, so large misses are penalized disproportionately (RMSE \(\geq\) MAE always, with equality only when every error has the same magnitude). Useful when big misses are costlier than the sum of many small ones; the gap between RMSE and MAE is itself informative —a wide gap flags a few outlier errors rather than uniformly mediocre ones.

MAPE — Mean Absolute Percentage Error \[ \text{MAPE} = \frac{100\%}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left| \frac{y_i - \hat y_i}{y_i} \right| \]

Expresses error as a percentage of the actual value, making it scale-free —the only one of the three that can be meaningfully compared across tickers trading at very different price levels (AMZN around $250 vs. META around $700). This is why Section 8 reports MAPE as the primary comparison metric across models and companies.

04

Models evaluated

All seven models share the same target (return at t+1, except TimesFM) and the same validation protocol, but they differ in how they represent temporal dependence —and, in TimesFM's case, in whether they are fit to this data at all.

Statistical — ARIMA(p,d,q)

ARIMA follows the classical Box–Jenkins methodology: identify candidate orders (here, by scanning a bounded grid rather than visually inspecting the autocorrelation and partial-autocorrelation functions), estimate coefficients by maximum likelihood, and select among candidates with the Akaike Information Criterion, which trades off fit against complexity —a lighter penalty on extra parameters than BIC, appropriate when slight overfitting to noise is a smaller concern than under-parametrizing a comparison model. Evaluation uses one-step rolling forecasts: at each validation point the model is refit on all real history observed up to that point and projects one step ahead, the standard way to backtest a univariate model without ever letting it see the future.

Equation \[ \phi(B)\,(1-B)^d y_t \;=\; c + \theta(B)\,\varepsilon_t \]

\(B\) is the lag operator (\(By_t = y_{t-1}\)); \(\phi(B) = 1-\phi_1B-\cdots-\phi_pB^p\) is the autoregressive polynomial; \(\theta(B) = 1+\theta_1B+\cdots+\theta_qB^q\) the moving-average polynomial; \(\varepsilon_t\) is white noise. Differencing \(d\) times removes the unit root before \(\phi\) and \(\theta\) are fit on the now-stationary series.

Machine learning — Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM

All three are decision-tree ensembles over the technical and calendar variables, and each sits differently on the bias–variance tradeoff than a single deep tree. An unpruned regression tree has low bias but high variance (it overfits easily); Random Forest reduces that variance by averaging many trees grown on bootstrap resamples of the training data (bagging) while additionally randomizing the subset of features considered at each split —which decorrelates the trees further than bootstrap sampling alone, making the average more effective. Gradient boosting (XGBoost, LightGBM) instead reduces bias directly: each new tree is fit to the residual error of the ensemble built so far, so the ensemble as a whole can represent a far more flexible function than any single tree, at the cost of being more prone to overfitting without regularization. All three are included to separate whether performance differences come from the ensembling principle itself (bagging vs. boosting) or from implementation details specific to one library.

Random Forest — bagging \[ \hat r_{t+1} = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} T_b(\mathbf{x}_t) \]

Each \(T_b\) is a regression tree trained on a bootstrap sample of the training rows, with a random subset of the 25 technical/calendar variables available at each split; \(\mathbf{x}_t\) is the feature vector for day \(t\). Averaging trees fit on different random perturbations of the data reduces variance without increasing bias.

XGBoost / LightGBM — gradient boosting \[ \hat r_{t+1}^{(K)} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} f_k(\mathbf{x}_t), \qquad \mathcal{L}^{(k)} = \sum_{i} \ell\!\left(r_i,\ \hat r_i^{(k-1)} + f_k(\mathbf{x}_i)\right) + \Omega(f_k) \]

Each tree \(f_k\) is fit to correct the residual of the ensemble built so far, regularized by \(\Omega(f) = \gamma T + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda\lVert w\rVert^2\) (\(T\) = number of leaves, \(w\) = leaf weights) to control overfitting —the same spirit as ridge/lasso regularization in linear models. XGBoost's specific contribution over classical gradient boosting is to fit each tree using a second-order (Newton) approximation of the loss —both its gradient and its Hessian— which converges faster and more stably than using the gradient alone, and it grows trees level by level. LightGBM instead grows leaf-wise (best-first, always splitting whichever leaf reduces loss the most) over pre-binned histograms of the features, which is faster and often more accurate at an equal tree count, at a higher risk of overfitting on small data that LightGBM controls with its own leaf-count constraints.

Deep learning — GRU and Transformer

Both are from-scratch sequence models, trained only on this project's own data (no pretraining), that receive a window of the last 30 daily returns and produce the next one. Vanilla recurrent networks struggle over sequences this long because gradients backpropagated through many time steps tend to vanish or explode, effectively hiding early inputs in the window from the loss; the Gated Recurrent Unit addresses this with learned gates that let the network decide, at every step, how much of the past to keep versus overwrite —a lighter cousin of the LSTM that merges its forget and input gates into a single update gate, with comparable performance on many tasks and fewer parameters to fit. The Transformer removes recurrence altogether: self-attention lets every position in the window attend directly to every other position in a single matrix multiplication, so the path length between any two time steps is \(O(1)\) instead of growing with their distance as in a recurrent network —which both parallelizes training and tends to preserve longer-range dependencies. Both architectures are included to contrast the classical recurrent paradigm with the attention-based one that now dominates the sequence-modeling literature.

GRU — Gated Recurrent Unit \[ \begin{aligned} z_t &= \sigma(W_z x_t + U_z h_{t-1} + b_z) \\[2pt] r_t &= \sigma(W_r x_t + U_r h_{t-1} + b_r) \\[2pt] \tilde h_t &= \tanh\!\big(W_h x_t + U_h (r_t \odot h_{t-1}) + b_h\big) \\[2pt] h_t &= (1-z_t)\odot h_{t-1} + z_t \odot \tilde h_t \end{aligned} \]

\(x_t\) is the daily return at position \(t\) of the window; the update gate \(z_t\) decides how much of the previous state \(h_{t-1}\) to keep versus the candidate \(\tilde h_t\); the reset gate \(r_t\) controls how much of the past influences that candidate. The forecast is a linear layer over the final state: \(\hat r_{t+1} = W_o h_{30} + b_o\).

Transformer — self-attention \[ \mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V, \qquad Q=XW^Q,\ K=XW^K,\ V=XW^V \]

\(X\in\mathbb{R}^{30\times d}\) is the window of 30 returns with positional encoding added —attention alone is permutation-invariant, so without it the model could not tell a Monday from a Friday. This implementation uses 4 attention heads in parallel, each free to learn a different notion of "relevant past day"; their outputs are concatenated and linearly projected. Every position attends directly to the 29 others in one matrix step, with no sequential recursion; the forecast comes from a linear layer over the final position's representation.

Foundation model — TimesFM

TimesFM represents a fourth, more recent paradigm: instead of fitting a model from scratch to each ticker's roughly 900 training rows, it is pretrained once on a large, heterogeneous corpus of real-world time series spanning many domains (retail demand, web traffic, weather, finance, and more), then applied zero-shot to a series it has never seen —with no per-ticker fine-tuning and no hyperparameter search. It is the time-series analogue of the pretrain-then-zero-shot-inference paradigm that large language models popularized for text. Architecturally it is a decoder-only Transformer operating on patches of consecutive time steps rather than individual points, which shortens the effective sequence length the attention mechanism must cover and lets a single forward pass produce a genuinely multi-step forecast, rather than one value at a time.

This is also why TimesFM is the one model in this study that targets the price level directly instead of the return: before forecasting, it instance-normalizes each input window internally (subtracting and rescaling by that window's own statistics) and denormalizes the output afterward, so its notion of "range" is always relative to the specific series it is looking at, never to a fixed range memorized at training time. This is precisely the property Section 2 identified as missing from the tree ensembles and the from-scratch networks, and it is what lets TimesFM extrapolate safely on a stock making new highs it has never been trained on.

TimesFM — zero-shot forecast \[ \hat y_{t+1:\,t+h} = \mathrm{TimesFM}_{\theta}\!\big(y_{t-c+1:\,t}\big) \]

\(\theta\) denotes the pretrained weights, held frozen for every ticker in this study —unlike every other model in this section, no fitting step happens here. \(c\) is the context window length fed to the model (512 trading days in this project) and \(h\) the forecast horizon, produced natively in one pass. The model also outputs calibrated quantile forecasts alongside the point forecast used here —a natural basis for uncertainty bands not yet surfaced in the dashboard.

05

Hyperparameter optimization

Every model except ARIMA (whose only structural hyperparameter is its order, chosen by AIC as above) and TimesFM (which has none to tune, being used as a frozen pretrained model) is tuned with Bayesian optimization via Optuna, using a Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE). Rather than exhaustively scanning a grid or sampling uniformly at random, TPE fits two probability densities over the hyperparameter space —one conditioned on configurations that scored well so far, one on configurations that scored poorly— and samples new candidates more likely under the former relative to the latter, an approximation to maximizing expected improvement. Under an identical evaluation budget, this explores the promising regions of the search space far more efficiently than grid or random search —relevant here because every evaluation means retraining the full model on the training split.

06

Multi-step forecasting

Beyond the one-step evaluation, every model also produces a recursive forecast roughly one business month ahead: the prediction for t+1 is fed back in as if it had been observed, to generate the forecast for t+2, and so on. This is the standard way to extend a one-step model to a multi-step horizon, with a caveat worth stating explicitly: errors compound with every iteration, and for the tabular models there is no real future open/high/low/volume to condition on —they are approximated with the forecast close itself— so uncertainty grows markedly faster than in the one-step evaluation. ARIMA and TimesFM are the two exceptions that produce a genuinely multi-step forecast natively, in a single pass (ARIMA's forecast function, with widening confidence intervals; TimesFM's patch-based decoder) rather than by recursive rollout, which is one more reason their multi-step forecasts deserve somewhat more trust than the recursively-rolled-out ones from the tabular and small recurrent models. The forecast is reported as an exploratory reference, not a calibrated confidence interval.

07

News sentiment analysis

Recent headlines for each company are classified with FinBERT, a BERT variant pretrained on general text via masked-language modeling and then further pretrained on financial text for three-class sentiment classification (positive / negative / neutral) —an instance of transfer learning: the general language understanding acquired during pretraining is adapted to a narrower domain with comparatively little financial-text data. In this version of the system, sentiment is treated as an exploratory signal plotted alongside price, not as an input to the forecasting models; feeding it in as a regressor (e.g., the daily share of negative headlines) is the natural next extension.

FinBERT — classification \[ P(c\mid x) = \mathrm{softmax}\big(W\,\mathrm{BERT}(x)_{[\text{CLS}]} + b\big)_c, \qquad c \in \{\text{positive, negative, neutral}\} \]

\(\mathrm{BERT}(x)_{[\text{CLS}]}\) is the contextual representation of the classification token after passing headline \(x\) through BERT's self-attention layers (the same mechanism as the Transformer above, here bidirectional and pretrained on financial text); the final linear layer projects that representation to three logits, and softmax turns them into probabilities.

This is, in itself, an informative result: at a one-day horizon on these four stocks, the choice of algorithm matters less than the statistical specification of the problem —consistent with the well-documented difficulty of beating a random walk at short horizons. TimesFM's result is the more striking version of the same point: it reaches parity with six models that were each individually tuned on this data, without seeing a single training example from any of these four tickers.

09

Continuous updates and visualization

A daily job refreshes prices and headlines; a separate weekly job retrains all seven models and regenerates both the historical fit and the forward forecast. Decoupling the two means the dashboard's real-price line is never more than a day stale, without paying the cost of refitting seven Bayesian-optimized models daily. An interactive dashboard lets users compare, for one or several companies at once, the real price against each model's historical fit and forward forecast, overlaid with the daily share of negative headlines, alongside a "Last training" indicator showing when the models currently shown were last refit.
